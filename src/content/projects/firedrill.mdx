<h3>The project</h3>
<p>Six of us had one semester to design, machine and weld a vehicle powered only by a cordless drill, then race it in the Run Off endurance event. The rules: under $200, under 50 lb, under 7 ft, at least three wheels, and at least three custom-machined parts including the frame. I was the project manager. This page is less about the trike and more about how we got it built: how the team came together, how we worked, and what I'd change.</p>
</AlternatingBlock>

<div class="single-col wide">
<h3>Building the team on purpose</h3>
<p>We didn't wait to get assigned a team. Our lead CAD engineer and I sat down and recruited one, looking for a mix of strengths instead of just friends. I'd worked with one of our manufacturing engineers before and knew she was a strong designer. We also found a student who had spent two summers as a test engineer, so we knew we had our testing lead before day one. By the first class, roles mostly sorted themselves out because everyone was already in a spot they wanted. Nobody else wanted to be PM, so I took it.</p>
</div>

<PullQuote accentColor="#b0522f">
One person driving, everyone else supporting.
</PullQuote>

<div class="single-col wide">
<h3>How we worked</h3>
<p>We ran a hybrid plan. The course deliverables were fixed milestones, laid out for the whole semester in our planning document in week three. The work to hit each one ran as a short sprint: one person owned it and drove it, and everyone else supported in a structured way. Every CAD part had a named owner, so when our lead CAD engineer was deep in the frame assembly and texted the group "I need the sprocket adapter in two hours," I knew it was mine and had it done right after class.</p>
<p style="margin-top:14px">We also set a team deadline 48 hours before every real one. I was learning about scheduling and buffers in my project management class at the same time, so I could take something from Monday's lecture and use it with the team on Tuesday.</p>
</div>

<div class="single-col wide">
<span class="alt-block__eyebrow" style="color:#b0522f">The receipts</span>
<h3>Every deliverable, from Gradescope</h3>
<p>Each bar runs from when we turned something in to when it was due. Early on we were submitting the same day. The Motors and Drills deliverable, a gear-ratio calculation, came down to the last morning and was stressful. After that the team agreed to really hold the 48-hour line, and from then on everything went in days early, including the manufacturing review revisions, which went in 13 days early.</p>
</div>

<h3>Putting people where they shine</h3>
<p>One of our CAD engineers was clearly the strongest at it, so I made him lead CAD and gave him real ownership of the model. He directed the frame work with our second CAD engineer and checked in with me when he needed to, instead of me managing every part. Our testing engineer and one manufacturing engineer teamed up on the drill plate, and the other manufacturing engineer took the pillow blocks and a few other parts. I took the sprockets, the hex drill axle, the sprocket adapter and all of the purchasing.</p>
</AlternatingBlock>

<div class="single-col wide">
<p>Leadership ended up shared, too. Our testing engineer became the go-to for assignments: when a document came up, she'd split it into sections and set the internal due date. That freed me up for the schedule, the shop and purchasing, and the team was better for having more than one person who could run things.</p>
</div>

<span class="alt-block__eyebrow" style="color:#b0522f">When something breaks</span>
<h3>Fix the problem, not the blame</h3>
<p>The day we went to put the head tube together, I had a feeling it wouldn't fit our fork. I tried it upstairs, and it didn't. My first instinct was to figure out whose part it was. I didn't send that text, because it wouldn't have helped anyone.</p>
<p style="margin-top:14px">Instead I took it to the shop. The first person I asked said we'd have to redesign the part, which would have cost days. I kept asking and found someone willing to cut it and try a faster fix. While I waited for him, I texted the team the new gusset length we'd need. Our lead CAD engineer had it designed by the time he got to the shop, we welded it in, and the problem was solved that same afternoon. Waiting an hour in the shop without lunch was worth not losing a week.</p>
</div>

<h3>The engineering</h3>
<p>The trike runs a 1 in A513 steel square-tube frame: sawed, bent, coped and MIG welded, with a curved S-tube up to the head tube. The drill mounts on a 3/16 in waterjet aluminum plate and drives a lathe-turned hex axle through a 10-tooth sprocket and a 60-tooth chainring, a 6:1 reduction sized from our drill speed and torque testing. The axle rides in milled aluminum pillow blocks with press-fit bearings. A donated kid's bike supplied the wheels, chain and fork.</p>
</AlternatingBlock>

  <h3>By the numbers</h3>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Budget:</strong> $172.35 spent of $200. We predicted $149 at the manufacturing review; sprockets and hardware ran higher, and donated and provided parts covered the gap.</li>
    <li><strong>Custom parts:</strong> frame, drill plate, pillow blocks, hex drill axle, modified drill sprocket, sprocket adapter, trigger block and dropouts</li>
    <li><strong>Schedule:</strong> shop availability and drawing redlines pushed frame welding back about two weeks from our own plan, and the buffer absorbed it. No deadline was missed.</li>
  </ul>

<div class="single-col wide">
<span class="alt-block__eyebrow" style="color:#b0522f">Looking back</span>
<h3>What I'd do differently</h3>
<ul class="fd-lessons">
  <li><strong>Schedule the wait.</strong> I planned shop time but not time waiting for the shop. The schedule held anyway because it had slack, but next time I'd plan for it on purpose.</li>
  <li><strong>Plan the build day.</strong> On our first welding day all five of us showed up without a clear plan, and there was a lot of standing around. Fifteen minutes with a manufacturing lead the day before would have fixed it.</li>
  <li><strong>Assign strengths deliberately.</strong> Our testing engineer ran assignments well because it played to her strengths. Next time I'd make that call explicitly on day one instead of letting it happen on its own.</li>
</ul>
</div>
